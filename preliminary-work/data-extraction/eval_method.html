The evaluation method — named travelers

Part II asks for one record per named traveler. This page covers the first field only, the name: how model rows are paired with ground-truth travelers, and how well the names were read. The other eight fields are scored right or wrong (eval_guidelines.html, equivalences.html). The method comes first (§1–6), then the evidence behind it (§7–10). Every figure is computed by build_eval_method.py.

1. Two questions

1. How many named travelers are on the page? Answerable without reading a single name correctly; Part I asks it directly.
2. Who were they? Each traveler once, with the name as written. A traveler who arrives and departs in the same week appears in both columns of the register but is one person, and one row.

A model can answer one well and the other badly, so each gets its own measure: the count for the first question, and matching (F1) and transcription (name CER) for the second.

2. Method

  • Count. Distinct names a model reports per page, shown as the range over 10 runs, against the ground-truth count.
  • Match. Each model row is paired with at most one ground-truth traveler, if their similarity is at least 0.70: the larger of the Ratcliff-Obershelp ratio and token overlap (§7). Titles, articles and places of origin are removed from the model's name first, and the best reading wins. Precision, recall and F1 count these pairs. A traveler listed twice matches once; the second row counts against precision.
  • Transcription. Character error rate on the name (edits / ground-truth length, micro-averaged), plus the share of names read exactly. Here it is computed over a one-to-one assignment with no similarity floor, so badly read names stay in. The name CER on summary.html covers matched pairs only — for GPT in the naive condition, 0.23 there against 0.44 here.
  • Leftover rows. Rows beyond that assignment are classified: double-listed, two travelers in one row, fragment, bad read, or fabricated. (summary.html §1.2 classifies every row that failed the match, so its counts are larger.)

5. Why this method

  • Finding and reading are different skills. GPT in the naive condition scores F1 0.471, yet on page 39 it finds 9–10 of 10 travelers; it misreads them (CER 0.57, against Gemini 3.1's 0.12).
  • Ratcliff-Obershelp to match, CER to measure reading. CER penalizes added text as heavily as wrong text, so as a matching test it rejects a correct name with a title or origin attached (the cer gate column).
  • Removing titles and origins never lowers a score, but it is not free. The name as written is always tried too. The removal also lifts some wrong readings over the threshold (the Tornielli and Mazuchelli cases in §8), and stripping unconditionally would damage Ernesto di Bargh, De Flamarin and Salamon Cormos, where the connective or the place is part of the name.
  • Row order is not required. The prompt does not fix a reading order. Enforcing one costs Gemini 2.5 (naive) 0.065 F1, and no other model and condition more than 0.023.

6. Limits

  • The no-floor assignment is bookkeeping for CER, not a claim that two names are the same person.
  • CER is stable over a whole cell and noisy on a single short name (§9).
  • Whether a traveler sits in the arrival or the departure column is not evaluated. Listing the same traveler in both is (§2).

7. The two measures

Both are computed on the reduced readings of the model's name (§2), and the best reading wins.

  • Match test — the larger of the Ratcliff-Obershelp ratio, 2·M / T (T is the combined length of both names, M the characters in matching blocks), and token overlap, |A∩B| / max(|A|,|B|). A pair at 0.70 or above can match; the value is then discarded.
  • CER — levenshtein(gt, model) / len(gt): edits per ground-truth character.

They agree on plain misreadings and part on length: the match test's combined length absorbs added text, CER's ground-truth length does not.
